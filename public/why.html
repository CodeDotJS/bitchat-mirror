We do not believe in internet censorship

The state treats the internet as a switch it owns

India has made deliberate internet shutdowns ordinary. It has written rules for the kill switch, automated takedowns of speech and software, and — when a tool still works after the network is cut — moved to erase the install path. This page is the record, with sources. The mirror is what you do about the last part.

Where it hits

Not a national kill switch — a habit

Indian shutdowns are usually state- or district-level, not a single nationwide cut. That makes them easy to dismiss as local — and easier to repeat. In 2024, people in 16 states and territories were hit; Manipur (21), Haryana (12), and Jammu & Kashmir (12) led. [6] Of those 84 incidents, 41 were protest-related and 23 tied to communal violence. [6] In 2025, 12 states and territories still saw shutdowns during protests, conflict, communal violence, and religious holidays. [4] [5]

Access Now’s STOP dataset (920 India rows, 2016–2025) shows the habit in the grain of the data: 555 incidents hit a single city, county, or village; 512 were ordered by a state government and 276 by local government. [35] SFLC.in’s separate tracker — running since 2012 — puts Jammu & Kashmir at 449 cumulative shutdowns, the highest of any Indian jurisdiction, inside a national total of 930 (live counters move; these figures are from my archived copy). What a blackout does to people

Beyond the tally

Manipur, 2023 — clinics and classrooms

Access Now documented a statewide disruption lasting about 212 days, affecting roughly 3.2 million people through 44 published orders. [9] [30] The KeepItOn 2023 report notes the impact was especially severe for women trying to document gender-based violence while the network was dark. [30]

In Churachandpur, radiologist Dr Ngaijavung spent months dispatching radiology reports by hand because the internet was gone. [23] A qualitative Indian Journal of Medical Research study of postgraduate trainees (21 in-depth interviews at two tertiary hospitals) found disrupted consultant opinions, delayed patient-report sharing, broken hand-offs, and blocked access to study materials. [21]

A July 2023 cross-sectional survey of 135 medical students in Imphal found 93.3% said the shutdown affected daily activities, 83.7% said it affected their studies, and 71.1% rated the experience as bad. [22] A separate survey of 401 college students across eight colleges reported 90% felt anxious and 69.1% felt depressed during the shutdown. [28]

Jammu & Kashmir — 552 days

After August 2019, the region lived through weeks of near-total blackout and then prolonged no-to-low (2G) access. Full 4G returned only after 552 days — among the longest communications restrictions recorded in a democracy. [7] [8]

Students travelled out of the Valley to fill exam and admission forms; businesses and schools stalled while the rest of the country moved online. [29]

The bill

Top10VPN’s cost model of major Indian restrictions put losses at about $184.3 million in 2022, $585.4 million in 2023 (with 5,655 blackout hours — Manipur dominated), and $322.9 million in 2024 (~2,920 hours; ~67.7 million users affected). [41] [10] [42] Earlier ICRIER research put the 2012–2017 cost near $3.04 billion. [18] These are economic models — not the same as Access Now’s incident counts. Different methods, same direction of harm.

The official excuse

“Stop the rumours”

Governments often justify shutdowns as public safety — including preventing “misinformation” and hate speech. Access Now’s KeepItOn reporting treats those stated reasons as frequently disproportionate or ineffective covers for control. [30]

The STOP ledger itself records the gap between stated justification and coded cause. Of India’s 920 deliberate shutdowns since 2016, authorities cited “fake news / hate speech / incendiary content / promoting violence” in 283 cases — more than any other justification category. Access Now’s coders marked the actual cause in those same rows as communal violence in 115 cases and protests in 81. Another 225 shutdowns were justified as public safety / restoring order. [35]

Ethnographic research on an Indian shutdown (published in the International Journal of Communication) found the opposite of the official story: cutting the network removed verification and fact-checking tools. People still received rumours by phone and word of mouth — without the means to check them. The author concludes the shutdown “succeeded in increasing the spread of misinformation and reduced the agency to counteract it.” [27]

That is the paradox of entitlement: silence the channel, then blame the dark for the rumours that grow in it.

How the switch works

Rules on paper · practice on the ground

The Court drew a line

In Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020), the Supreme Court held that freedom of speech and expression, and the freedom to practise trade or business, using the medium of the internet, are protected under Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g). Restrictions must meet reasonableness and proportionality. Indefinite suspension under the Suspension Rules is not permitted. [12] The Court did not create a free-standing “right to internet access.”

After that judgment, India kept leading the world in shutdown counts — 116 in 2023, 84 in 2024, 65 in 2025. [9] [6] [4]

Suspension Rules, 2024

On 22 November 2024 the Centre notified the Telecommunications (Temporary Suspension of Services) Rules, 2024 under the Telecommunications Act, 2023 — replacing the 2017 rules. [24] [26] Orders must be in writing, limited to a specified duration not exceeding fifteen days, and define geography and service type; a review committee is to meet within five days. [25] [26]

SFLC.in calls the review body an in-house bureaucratic committee and notes there is still no duty to publish shutdown orders in real time — a “performative upgrade” risk: new language, same darkness. [24]

The other lever

Takedowns · code · VPNs

Cutting mobile data is only one tool. The other is compelling intermediaries. Section 69A of the IT Act is the older, more procedurally constrained blocking route. Section 79(3)(b) — accelerated through the Home Ministry’s Sahyog portal (October 2024) — pushes faster takedown notices to platforms. [13] RTI data showed 130 Sahyog notices between October 2024 and early April 2025; related disclosures cited 426 §79(3)(b) notices covering over one lakh pieces of content in a broader year. [14] A later SFLC.in RTI reply counted 179 Sahyog-portal requests to intermediaries across the window they asked about. [40] X called Sahyog a “censorship portal.” The Karnataka High Court upheld it in 2025. [15]

Under §69A, the volume is no longer a trickle. Parliament was told in 2023 that MeitY averaged about 6,000 blocking orders a year and had blocked 36,838 URLs between January 2018 and October 2023. Senior ministry officials later told The Indian Express the tally rose to roughly 12,600 orders in 2024 and 24,300 in 2025 — with about 60% of recent orders aimed at X, and the emergency clause (interim block pending 48-hour committee ratification) “increasingly used.” [36]

Circumvention is in the crosshairs too. KeepItOn’s 2025 India section records that on 29 December 2025 authorities in Jammu & Kashmir imposed a roughly two-month ban on unauthorized VPN use, with reports of about 800 users penalized. [5]

Reporters Without Borders ranks India 151st of 180 in its 2025 World Press Freedom Index (2026 listing: 157th). [31] When the network and the platforms both go dark, independent reporting is what dies first.

When offline tools become the target

23 July 2026 · I4C
Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre IT Act §79(3)(b) · three-hour deadline

GitHub ordered to disable bitchat — because it works during shutdowns

After years of cutting connectivity, the state confronted a tool that routes around the cut — peer-to-peer Bluetooth mesh messaging without a central server path the notice could simply switch off — and reached for the distribution channel. The order landed while mobile data around Delhi student protests was already being suspended. [1] [2] [3]

“The government of India does not like technologies like Bitchat and wants it taken down.”

— Jack Dorsey, publishing the I4C notice [1]

What the notice itself argued

  • Anonymous mesh without registration or central logs “significantly impedes lawful interception, attribution, and investigation.” [2]
  • The architecture could keep people communicating during internet shutdowns. [2] [3]
  • Three hours to comply — or risk losing intermediary safe-harbour under Rule 7. [3]

The grievance is not a crime inside a binary. The grievance is survival of communication after the state flips the network switch.

Why a mirror

Mesh messaging only matters if you can still obtain the binary. GitHub, app stores, and CDNs are single points of failure under §79(3)(b). [2] [3] This site keeps signed Android builds, checksums, and tagged source commits reachable outside a funnel that can be ordered dark in three hours.
